How It Works

1
Buy chips. Deposit USDT into the contract; it credits your chip balance 1:1 at a fixed rate. Nothing moves again until you sit at a table.
2
Create or join a table. The first player to sit picks the stakes from a fixed ladder and a buy-in anywhere from 50× to 200× that table's big blind; whatever they choose becomes the table's fixed buy-in for everyone who joins after. Your balance must cover it before you're even allowed to pick that stake. If your stack is later ground down below 10× the big blind, you're automatically sat out of hands (not removed from your seat) until you top back up — see "What happens if my stack gets too short?" in the FAQ. The first time you sit, your wallet also asks for one approval that starts your gameplay session (it pops up by itself — there's no button to press).
3
The hand is dealt automatically. These things move out of your table stack at this exact moment: the blind (if you're in the small or big blind seat); on your first hand after sitting down at a table that has already played hands, a full entry big blind (see the FAQ); and, only if three or more players are actually dealt into this particular hand, a 5-big-blind "reveal bond" from every dealt-in player (capped at whatever's left of their stack after the blind — with the 10×BB minimum to be dealt in, that cap never comes into play). Any hand with exactly two players holds back no bond at all — a heads-up table always qualifies, and so does a six-max table on a hand where only two seats are actually dealt in — see the FAQ for why. The bond isn't a fee — it's refunded to your stack when the hand resolves. It is only forfeited if you are marked stalled (you missed a protocol deadline) or the hand reaches showdown and you skip your shuffle audit — in short, if you don't complete the hand, for any reason, you lose the bond. A forfeited bond goes to the site's fee recipient, not to the other players.
4
The deal. Every player posts their keys with a proof, then the players shuffle and re-mask the encrypted deck one after the other while also posting an escrow of their lock key. Then everyone swaps in their personal lock keys and removes their locks from the other players' hole cards, all at the same time. Your browser does all of it automatically and it takes a few seconds; the status line shows progress. Nobody — including the contract — can read the deck.
5
Hole cards. Every other player publishes a proof-carrying share that removes their lock from your hole cards (they still can't see them — your own lock is still on). You finish the decryption privately in your browser.
6
Betting. Standard No-Limit Hold'em action, turn by turn. Nobody can act on a street until that street's community card is actually revealed — no blind betting. Every turn has a hard 7-second clock, visible as a countdown, so hands play at live-poker speed. On top of that each player has a 15-second time bank per hand for the odd street that needs more thought: if you take longer than 7 seconds, the extra time is taken from your bank automatically (no button to press). Once the 7 seconds and whatever bank you have left run out, the hand is folded for you automatically.
7
Showdown. Everyone still in reveals their shuffle seed and their own hole-card keys (done automatically by your browser, within 5 seconds, so keep the tab open and in front). The contract replays each shuffle and checks each card before paying out the pot; this page can independently re-check it too.
8
Rake. Currently 3% of contested pots (owner-adjustable, hard-capped at 5% by the contract itself), uncapped per-pot (always exactly that percentage, however large the pot) — never taken from a hand that's voided or where blinds are simply returned. Rake is computed with no fractional chip ever silently dropped: any leftover fraction of a chip from one pot's rake carries forward and is folded into the next raked pot's calculation. A rate change only ever applies to pots raked after that change, never retroactively.
9
Nobody can stall a hand. Every player escrows their lock key while the deck is shuffled. A player who goes silent is folded and, if the hand had three or more players, forfeits their reveal bond; the others open that lock in about one block, and the hand plays on. With only two players in the hand, there's no escrow and no bond — the other player simply wins.
10
Be at the table when the players are. Player traffic comes in three daily 5-minute windows: 04:00, 12:00 and 20:00 UTC. Each window lasts exactly 5 minutes from the start time. Our site is new, so players need to converge at these times — sit down sharp at the start time for the best chance of finding a game.

Can the contract, or the site operator, see my cards?

No. The deck is shuffled and masked by the players themselves — client-side, in each player's own browser — before it ever reaches the contract. Cards are elliptic-curve points; the contract only stores masked values, verifies zero-knowledge proofs, and learns a card only when it is a community card that the whole table has opened. As long as one player's shuffle is honest, no other player and no group of other players can learn your cards.

Can I refresh the page or switch tabs during a hand?

No. Everything is automatic: your browser shuffles, unlocks cards and reveals your hand at showdown by itself, and the showdown reveal has only 5 seconds. Do not refresh the screen or switch to other tabs or apps while a hand is in progress, otherwise the automatic steps can be paused by the browser and you may miss a deadline, be folded and lose the hand (plus your reveal bond, if the hand has three or more players). Stay on this tab until the hand is over.

What if a hand gets stuck and my chips are locked?

Every stage of a hand has a short clock (5 to 20 seconds depending on the step, and 7 seconds per betting action). A player who misses one is marked stalled by anyone: folded, chips left in the pot, and the other players break their escrowed key open automatically, in about one block, so the hand plays on. If nobody at all is around for 15 minutes past a deadline, the hand is cleared automatically as soon as any seated player opens the page again (there is no button for it): the hand is chopped — every player still active gets their own chips back plus an equal share of what folded and stalled players put in, and nothing goes to the house (unless every player in the hand was at fault), apart from the reveal bonds of players who were marked stalled.

Where does my money actually sit?

Chips are bought with USDT and held in the contract itself, not a company ledger — they only ever move into a pot when a hand actually starts, under publicly readable rules. It only leaves your free balance when you sit at a table (locked as your table stack), and only leaves your table stack when a hand starts — at that moment blinds are posted into the pot (plus, on your first hand after sitting down at a running table, an entry big blind), and, only if three or more players are dealt into that hand, a 5 BB reveal bond is separately held back from every dealt-in player. A hand with exactly two players — heads-up, or a six-max table currently down to two — holds back no bond at all. See "What's the reveal bond?" below for what that bond is and when you get it back.

I'm joining a table that's already playing — do I have to post a blind?

Yes. If a table has already dealt at least one hand, a new player posts one big blind on the first hand they are dealt into, wherever they sit. If your seat is the big blind, that is your normal blind and nothing extra is taken; if it is the small blind, you top it up to a full big blind; anywhere else you post a full big blind. It is a live blind, like any other: it counts as your bet and you keep the option to check. This exists so nobody can sit down in early position, leave before the blinds reach them, and come back again and again without ever paying a blind. Leaving and sitting down again (even at the same table) means posting again. You can join in the middle of a hand: that hand carries on without you and you are dealt in from the next one, as long as your stack is at least 10× the big blind and you aren't sitting out. Players who sat down before the table's very first hand pay nothing extra.

What happens if my stack gets too short?

If your table stack drops below 10× the table's big blind — from losing hands, not from anything at buy-in time, since the minimum buy-in for any table is already 50× BB — you're automatically sat out. Your seat stays yours and nothing is taken from you; you simply aren't dealt into the next hand (or any hand after that) until you top back up. Once you're below that 10×BB line, you can't just dribble in a small amount that leaves you still short — a top-up has to bring your stack all the way back up to at least 50×BB in one go. The moment that top-up lands, you're back in for the very next hand — there's nothing else to click. At a table dealing three or more players into a hand, this floor exists partly because being dealt in also means posting the reveal bond (see below), which is 5× the big blind — the 10×BB floor is double that, so a player dealt in at the minimum always has chips left behind after the blind and the bond (at least 4× BB), the bond can never swallow a short stack's entire remainder, and it is still capped at whatever a player actually has, so it can't put anyone below zero. Any hand with exactly two players holds back no bond, but the same 10×BB floor still applies, so a hand is never dealt to a stack too short to actually play. Leaving a hand unfinished always costs something real, never pennies.

What if I go AFK and stop acting, but don't leave the table?

Hands are dealt automatically, so if you simply stop acting you'll be dealt in and time out: betting clocks auto-check (if free) or fold you, and if you stop answering protocol steps you are marked stalled and, if the hand had three or more players, lose your bond too. If you want a break, tick Sit out before you go. If you time out of a hand you're sat out automatically, and if you then stay away for 3 more hands you are removed from your seat and your stack goes back to your chip balance (nothing is taken; you can sit down again, under the same short stack lock as leaving). A table left idle for a long stretch can be closed by the other players, and your chips go back to your balance.

What's the reveal bond?

Only charged when three or more players are actually dealt into a hand. 5 big blinds (capped at whatever's left of your stack after the blind), held back from every dealt-in player's table stack the instant such a hand starts — on top of, and separate from, the blinds. It's not a fee: it's refunded straight back to your stack when the hand resolves, in every case except two: (1) you were marked stalled (you missed a protocol deadline: shuffle key, shuffle, escrow, swap, a lock removal, or the showdown reveal), or (2) the hand reached showdown and you never audited your shuffle. In plain terms: not completing a hand, for any reason, costs you the bond. A forfeited bond goes to the site's fee recipient, not to the other players (they didn't win that stack), and an on-chain RevealBondForfeited event makes it public. It is not part of the pot and is never counted as rake. It's sized at 5 BB because that's the level that actually deters players from walking away mid-hand — see "How to play — read this first" at the top of the page for the safe way to sit down and stand up without ever paying it.

Any hand with exactly two players collects no reveal bond at all — no matter what kind of table it is. That covers a heads-up table (always two players) and, just as much, a six-max table on a hand where only two of its seats are actually dealt in. The bond funds the deterrent against a player abandoning a hand while their lock key sits in escrow for the others to break open, and it guards against a colluding sub-group trying to exploit that — and both the escrow and the collusion risk need a third player to even exist (see "Can players collude or chip-dump?"). With only two players there's no escrow, no swap, and a stall simply ends the hand in your opponent's favour, so nothing is held back to begin with; walking away mid-hand still costs you the pot, just not a separate bond.

How long do I have to act? What is the time bank?

Every betting action (check, call, bet, raise, fold) has a hard clock, 7 seconds by default. That's deliberate: with no limit, hands can take far longer than they would at a live table, so the pace is set to roughly live-poker speed and enforced by the contract itself. The fee recipient can adjust this clock (and the time bank below) if it turns out to be too tight for players, within a fixed 3-120 second range — a change only applies to hands dealt after it, never to a hand already in progress.

To cover the occasional street that genuinely needs more thought, each player also has a time bank per hand, 15 seconds by default. It works automatically, with no button to press: if you act within the base clock, nothing is used. If you take longer, the extra seconds are deducted from your bank. The bank doesn't refill during a hand and resets to full at the start of the next one; each player has their own. While you're on the clock the page shows the seconds left, and your remaining bank and the other players' banks are shown next to it.

If both the base clock and your remaining bank run out, your hand is folded automatically and the other players play on (if only one is left, they win the pot). Because every action is a blockchain transaction, it has to land before the deadline — so don't cut it close, keep a stable connection, and keep some POL in your wallet for gas. This clock applies to betting decisions; the shuffle, card-reveal and showdown-key steps have their own, longer clocks.

What happens if another player disconnects mid-hand?

It depends on when, but the hand always finishes. On a betting turn, a player who exceeds the 7-second clock plus their time bank is auto-checked (if free) or folded. If a player stops answering a protocol step (posting keys, shuffling, removing their lock from a card), anyone can mark them stalled: they are folded, their chips stay in the pot, and the other players combine the escrow shares of their lock key within one block, so the hand plays on immediately. A player who has already folded publishes their lock key at once (automatically) and owes nothing after that, so folding and closing the tab never holds anyone up, and a hand that is down to two players needs no escrow at all: if one of them vanishes, the other wins. Before betting starts no card has been seen, so the hand is simply aborted: the stalled player's blind stays forfeit (and, if the hand had three or more players, their bond too) and the table re-deals without them. If only one player is left, they win the pot immediately. Only if two players vanish at the same moment is the hand chopped.

What's the rake?

Currently a fixed 3%, uncapped per-pot (no maximum, however large the pot), taken only from pots that are actually won at fold, showdown, or forfeit — never from a voided hand where blinds are simply returned. The rake is owner-adjustable (the fee recipient can call setRakeBps), but the contract itself hard-caps it at 5% — setRakeBps reverts for anyone, including the fee recipient, above that ceiling, and a rate change only ever applies to pots raked after that change, never retroactively to rake already collected. It's the same rate for every table, at every stake, at any given moment. Everything you see and play with — your stack, the pot, blinds, payouts, and rake — is always a whole number of chips, because 1 chip = 0.01 USDT and the contract has no way to pay out or collect a fraction of a chip. That's the reason rake needs a rounding rule at all: on a pot where the rake percentage isn't a whole number of chips, that specific hand's rake is rounded down to the nearest whole chip. For example, at the current 3% rate, on a 333-chip (3.33 USDT) pot: 3% of 333 chips = 9.99 chips, so the rake actually taken that hand is 9 chips (0.09 USDT) and the winner(s) receive 324 chips (3.24 USDT). The leftover 0.99 of a chip isn't lost — it's carried forward and folded into the rake calculation for the next raked pot, where it may push that hand's rake up by an extra chip once enough fractional rake has accumulated. Over a large enough sample of hands at a stable rate, total rake collected converges to exactly that rate's share of total raked volume — no value is ever permanently dropped, it's just a matter of which hand a given fraction of a chip's rake ends up landing on.

What's the "card key" for?

It's a random 16-digit hex code the page creates for you automatically the first time you connect a wallet, and keeps in this browser's local storage (IndexedDB). It's never sent anywhere and you never have to type or remember it. It deterministically generates each hand's encryption keys, so a page refresh in the middle of a hand is recoverable. It is NOT your wallet's private key. One thing to know: it lives in this browser only, so if you clear the site's data or switch browser or device in the middle of a hand, that hand's keys can't be regenerated and the hand can only time out (if the hand had three or more players, you could forfeit your reveal bond). Between hands, a fresh key is harmless because every hand's keys are derived separately.

Where is my hand history stored?

Two places. The detailed version — with your own hole cards, since those are yours to keep — lives in this browser's IndexedDB, locally, never transmitted anywhere; clearing your browser data will erase it, so use the Download button in the History panel if you want to keep a copy. Separately, a compact summary of every hand (table, players, board, pot, rake, winners) is written permanently on-chain the moment it resolves, addressable forever by its hand number — see "Check a hand by number" in the History panel. That on-chain copy survives even if you clear your browser, switch devices, or never opened this page during the hand at all.

What is a "hand number"?

Every hand ever played on this contract, at any table, gets a permanent, never-reused hand number the moment it starts (shown in the table view once a hand is in progress). Because the contract only keeps a table's in-progress hand data around until the next hand starts there, this number is what lets anyone — including support, if you submit feedback — look up exactly what happened in a specific past hand afterward, via "Check a hand by number" in the History panel.

Can I actually verify a hand myself?

Yes — your browser checks every showdown hand for you automatically just after it resolves (the result shows under "Last check"), and you can also click "Verify This Hand" in the table view before the next hand is dealt — the contract reuses that storage once a new hand starts, which now happens a few seconds after each hand. It independently replays the entire shuffle/encryption chain in your browser using the same math the contract itself uses.

What if I lose a hand because of a software bug?

We build and test in good faith, checking the code rigorously before every release, so this shouldn't happen — but if it does, and you lose a hand or any part of a pot to a bug in this software, that loss is yours to bear, not the site's or its operators'. We fix issues as they're reported, but this is software running real money on a public blockchain with no support desk that can reverse a transaction once it's confirmed. Only play at stakes you'd genuinely accept losing outright to a bug or a missed timeout — treat every buy-in that way, not just the ones you expect to lose at the table.

Two things are on you, not the software: keep a few extra POL in your wallet, separate from your USDT buy-in, to cover gas for every action (checking, betting, revealing cards), and play on a stable internet connection. Every betting turn has a hard clock — if you don't act in time, whether from running out of gas, a dropped connection, or anything else, your hand is auto-folded, and that's final even if you were holding the winning cards.

If something goes wrong, you can file a permanent, publicly-visible on-chain report, optionally tied to the exact hand it happened in.

Where's the gaming license and RNG certificate?

There isn't one, because there's no licensable business here to certify: no company, no site, no server, and no in-house operator running this game on anyone's behalf. What you're interacting with is a smart contract — a public utility deployed on the blockchain — that runs autonomously exactly as written, the same way for every player, forever, with no one able to switch it off or change how it behaves. There's no RNG to certify in the traditional sense either: cards aren't generated by a server rolling dice, they're shuffled and encrypted jointly by the players themselves using the elliptic-curve mental-poker protocol described above, and that process is independently verifiable by anyone via "Verify This Hand" — a stronger guarantee than a certificate asking you to trust a third party's audit. There's no KYC because there's no operator to collect or hold it; deposits and withdrawals move automatically to and from your own wallet through the contract, with nobody in the loop to approve, delay, or freeze them. None of this makes real-money poker legal everywhere — gambling law is set by your jurisdiction, not by this contract, and it varies widely. It's on you, individually, to check and follow the law wherever you are before you play.

Why offer both heads-up and six-max tables?

Both are supported. Heads-up tables have exactly two players, so collusion is meaningless there — which is also why heads-up tables hold back no reveal bond, and a six-max table gets the same exemption whenever a given hand only has two players actually dealt in (see "What's the reveal bond?"). Six-max tables are more fun, but once three or more players are actually in a hand they reintroduce the problems every poker site has: players can collude, soft-play each other, or chip-dump. Nothing in the cryptography can prevent that — the deal is provably fair, the players are not provably honest — and there is no operator to ban anyone. Treat six-max like any online table: play with people you'd trust, and assume other seats might be coordinating.

Can players collude or chip-dump?

Whenever a hand has only two players — a heads-up table, or a six-max table dealt down to two — no: collusion needs a third player to cheat, which is also why that hand collects no reveal bond — there is no escrow-based abandonment for a bond to guard against. Once a hand has three or more players, yes, collusion is possible in principle — as at any poker table. What the protocol does guarantee is that colluders cannot see anyone else's cards or manipulate the shuffle: even if five players share everything they know, the sixth player's hole cards stay hidden while that player's own lock is on them.

Why is there no "Enable gameplay" button? What is the gameplay session?

Every action inside a hand (shuffling, unlocking cards, checking, betting, folding, revealing) has to be signed within seconds, so it is signed by a temporary session key that your browser creates for you, instead of asking your wallet for a pop-up every time. Only your real wallet can authorize that key, so one wallet approval can't be skipped — but you no longer have to find and press a button for it. The request appears by itself the moment you sit at a table (or return to a table you're already seated at). The session lasts 12 hours, and shortly before it ends your browser renews it automatically (one more approval; you can turn that off in Settings). The session key is not your wallet key: it can't withdraw your funds and can only play hands. It is funded with a small POL gas reserve that is sent back to your wallet when you leave the table or end the session. If you decline the wallet request, or it fails, we stop asking and a Retry gameplay setup button appears in the top bar.

Are hands dealt automatically? How do I take a break?

Yes — like a normal poker site. As soon as at least two seated players have enough chips (at least 10 big blinds each) and none is sitting out, the hand is dealt automatically, and a few seconds after each hand ends the next one is dealt by itself. Your browser also handles the keys, shuffle, escrow, card reveals and showdown for you, so during play the only thing you do is make your betting decisions. This needs the page to stay open; your gameplay session sets itself up automatically the first time you sit down (one wallet approval). To take a break, tick Sit out: the hand in progress finishes normally, then you are skipped until you untick it. You can stay sat out for 3 hands; after that you are removed from the seat and your stack returns to your chip balance.

What if a player refuses to finish a hand or walks away mid-hand?

They lose. Once cards are dealt, the hand always finishes: a player who goes silent is folded (their chips remain in the pot as dead money) and, if the hand had three or more players, forfeits their reveal bond too (any hand with exactly two players holds back no bond — see "What's the reveal bond?"). There is no way to dodge a losing hand — or a hand you simply don't like — by leaving: quitting costs you the pot every time, and the players still in collect. If nobody is left at all, the pot goes to the site's fee recipient. Their escrowed key shares (in a 3+ player hand) are what let the other players continue without them.

⚖ Provably Fair — the Proof

No dealer, no server, no operator who could ever see your hole cards before you do. Here's exactly how, step by step — and how to check it yourself.

1

Every seated player shuffles

Each player at the table (2 to 6) picks their own secret random shuffle seed and secret card keys, generated and held client-side in their own browser. No player ever tells anyone else their seed or keys — not even the contract.

2

The deck is masked in turn, then re-keyed

Cards are elliptic-curve points. Each player in turn shuffles the deck and re-masks every card with fresh randomness under the players' joint key, so a card can only be opened with everyone's help. Then every player swaps the shared shuffle key for their personal lock keys, proving in zero knowledge that they did exactly that and nothing else.

3

Nobody can predict the deal

As long as ONE player's shuffle is honest, nobody — no other player, no group of the other five, and not the contract — can learn which card sits where. Publishing a key never reveals anything: there is no public value from which a secret can be computed.

4

Cards open one lock at a time, in parallel

Every other player publishes a proof-carrying share that removes their lock from your hole cards; the shares commute, so all of them land at once. You finish the last step privately with a key that never leaves your browser until showdown.

5

Escrow: nobody can hold the game hostage

While the shuffle runs, each player splits their lock key into shares, one per other player, encrypted to each recipient, and the contract checks the shares add up to the key. If a player disappears, the others combine their shares in one block, the player is folded, and the hand carries on.

6

Anyone can verify it

At showdown every player still in reveals their shuffle seed; the contract replays each shuffle and checks every card against the hash-to-curve point. This page independently re-checks the same data in your browser.

Verify it yourself

This isn't a marketing claim — it's a real cryptographic check you can run yourself, right here in the browser, against the actual on-chain data. Open a table you've played, once a hand resolves, and click "Verify This Hand" before the next one is dealt (your browser also checks each showdown hand for you automatically; the contract reuses that storage slot once a new hand begins).

Why this matters

✓
No trusted dealer. A centralized poker site could — in principle — see every hand before you do. Here, the deck is never decrypted by the contract or any third party — only the players at the table open cards, and each player sees only their own hole cards.
✓

✓

The problem this solves: in-house server bots and insider access

Every centralized poker site is, structurally, a black box: its own servers deal every hand, its own database logs every hole card, and its own staff and software have full access to that data while a hand is still live. Players have raised this exact concern for as long as online poker has existed — that an operator (or a bot the operator controls) could sit at a table with real-time knowledge no other player has, or that an insider with database access could look at hands as they happen. Nobody outside the company can ever verify one way or the other. Even a completely honest operator is, by design, asking players to just take its word for it.

This table removes that trust requirement structurally, not by policy: there is no server. The deck is shuffled and encrypted entirely by the players sitting down — all the players at the table — using the elliptic-curve mental-poker protocol described above — no third party, including whoever deployed this contract, ever holds a key that could decrypt a hole card before its owner does. There is no "house" account, no privileged database, and no backend process a bot could be wired into, because the thing usually described as "the house" — a server with god-mode visibility into every hand — simply does not exist in this architecture. Funds sit in a smart contract with transparent, publicly readable rules (blind sizes, buy-ins, payout logic, and every state transition a hand goes through) rather than a company's ledger, and every hand's fairness is independently checkable by anyone — not just claimed by an operator — via "Verify This Hand."

What this does not claim: it removes the insider/house-advantage problem — nobody with privileged server access can see or steer your cards, because that privileged access doesn't exist to begin with. It cannot, and does not claim to, police what a player does on their own computer while playing — no dealing protocol, on-chain or off, can see or control that. That remains the same rules-and-etiquette matter it is in any poker game, online or live.

🃏 House Rules — read this first

This isn't like poker on a normal site. On a normal site a company's server deals the cards and you just click. Here there is no server and no dealer — all the players at the table deal every hand together, each with their own secret lock on the deck. That's what makes it provably fair, and it also changes how you need to play.

The one rule: once cards are dealt, every hand finishes. A player who goes silent is folded — their chips stay in the pot — and the hand plays on without them.

Every card is locked by every player, so it can only be opened when all of them take their lock off. To make sure nobody can hold a hand hostage by closing their browser, every player also escrows their lock key with the others while the deck is being shuffled. If someone goes silent, the rest combine their shares of that key automatically (about one block), the silent player is folded, and the hand carries on. Walk away, stall, or refuse to continue and you lose the pot — and, if the hand had three or more players, your reveal bond — to the players who stayed. That's what stops anyone quitting a hand they're losing. (Any hand with only two players needs no escrow: if one of them vanishes, the other simply wins — and that hand held back no reveal bond to begin with, whether it's a heads-up table or a six-max table dealt down to two.)

Hands with three or more players hold back a 5BB reveal bond. Here's how to join and leave without ever paying it.

Any hand you're dealt into alongside two or more other players holds back a 5×BB "reveal bond" from your stack (see the FAQ). A hand with just you and one opponent holds back none at all — that's true on a heads-up table, and just as true on a six-max table whenever a hand happens to deal in only two seats — collusion needs a third player, so there's nothing for a bond to guard against there. It's fully refunded the moment the hand resolves normally — joining and leaving the right way never costs you a cent of it. It's only forfeited if a hand starts and then doesn't finish because of you.

To join without risk: just sit down and deposit your stack. Nothing is held back until cards are actually dealt to you — and even then, the bond comes straight back as soon as that hand ends normally. There's no special timing to it.

To leave without risk: tick Sit out first. The hand you're currently in (if any) finishes completely — keep the tab open until it does — and no new hand is dealt to you after that. Only once you're sitting out (or between hands) press Leave table to cash out. Closing the browser, killing your connection, or walking away while a hand is live is the one thing that costs you the bond — there is no version of "just quitting" that's free here.

Why this is different from a normal poker site: on a centralized site, a house server deals every card and holds every player's hand; if you vanish, the server simply folds you and moves on — nobody else is affected. There is no server here. The other players at your table are the dealer: cards are locked with every seated player's own key, and unlocking each street needs all of them to act. If you disappear mid-hand, you're not just quitting on the house — the whole table has to sit through your timeout clock and (six-max) automatically break your key open before play can continue. That's real friction for everyone else at the table, not just you, which is exactly what the bond exists to discourage.

Play only with a connection and power you trust. A dropped connection or a power cut mid-hand looks identical, on-chain, to someone deliberately stalling — the contract can't tell the difference, so it's treated the same way: folded, and the bond is forfeited. If your internet or power isn't reliable, please don't sit at a table with real money on it — a single outage at the wrong moment costs you your bond, and repeated outages cost the other players real time and pot equity too.
